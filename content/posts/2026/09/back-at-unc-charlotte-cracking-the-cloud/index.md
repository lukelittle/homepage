On September 16th I was back at UNC Charlotte, giving my "Cracking the Cloud" talk to computing students again. It's the same talk that started all of this last year, and it felt good to walk back into a room full of students who are trying to figure out how to break into this industry.

This visit was a little different for me. I recently joined the UNC Charlotte Alumni Board of Directors, so this time I wasn't just a guest speaker passing through. I was coming back as someone who has a real stake in how these students do after they leave campus.

## Why I Keep Giving This Talk

When I first gave [Cracking the Cloud at UNC Charlotte]({{< relref "/posts/2025/12/cracking-the-cloud" >}}), the goal was simple: give students a realistic, actionable way to stand out in a job market that keeps getting tougher. Since then I've given versions of it elsewhere, including [at VCU]({{< relref "/posts/2026/03/speaking-at-vcu-cracking-the-cloud" >}}) earlier this year, and every time I update the deck, the core message gets more urgent, not less.

Early-career hiring is still tight. AI is still reshaping what a junior engineer's day looks like. And students can feel that. What they usually don't have is a plan.

That's what this talk is for.

## The 30-60-90 Plan, Still

The backbone of the talk hasn't changed, because it still works. I walked students through the same 30-60-90 plan:

**First 30 days: learn the fundamentals.** Pick up an entry-level cloud certification like AWS Cloud Practitioner. It isn't about collecting badges. It gets you past résumé filters, sure, but more importantly it gives you the vocabulary and mental models to actually build things.

**By 60 days: build something small.** A website, an API, a simple data pipeline. Anything that forces you to make real architectural decisions with real services. The AWS Free Tier means you can do this from a dorm room with nothing but a laptop.

**By 90 days: connect with people.** Go to meetups. Talk to alumni. Share what you built. This is the part students skip most often, and it's the part that most often turns into internships and offers.

The plan is intentionally simple. It turns "I don't know where to start" into a starting point.

## Projects and Your Personal Brand

I spent a good chunk of time on what happens after you build something. A project sitting in a private repo doesn't do much for you. A project you've written up, explained, and put somewhere a recruiter can find it does a lot.

This is why I keep pushing students to own their online presence. Earlier this year I put together a [guide to building your personal brand]({{< relref "/posts/2026/02/building-your-personal-brand-student-guide" >}}) along with a free template students can deploy to GitHub Pages in about ten minutes. You don't need to be a web developer. You just need a place where your work lives and a habit of writing about what you're learning.

Certifications get you noticed. Projects prove you can do the work. Writing about those projects shows you can communicate, and that's often what separates candidates with similar résumés.

## Relationships Still Matter Most

The last theme is the one I care about most. The cloud has democratized the tools, but it hasn't democratized the guidance. Students still need people who have been where they're trying to go.

That's a big part of why I joined the alumni board. I want to help build more of those connections between UNC Charlotte students and the people already working in the industry. Coming back to give this talk is one small piece of that.
